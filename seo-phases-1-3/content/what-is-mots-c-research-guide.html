MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded by a short open reading frame in the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene. It belongs to the family of mitochondrial-derived peptides and is studied in laboratory models of cellular metabolism, mitochondrial–nuclear communication and metabolic stress. This guide summarises the compound's identity, the pathways described in the peer-reviewed literature, and the documentation researchers should review before using a lot.

How MOTS-c was discovered

Mitochondrial DNA was long thought to encode only the 13 proteins of the electron-transport chain plus the rRNAs and tRNAs needed to make them. The identification of humanin, a peptide encoded within the 16S rRNA gene, suggested that further short open reading frames might exist. In 2015, Lee and colleagues reported a short open reading frame within the 12S rRNA encoding a 16-residue peptide, which they named MOTS-c [1].

Pathways described in the literature

Folate cycle and AMPK

In the original report, MOTS-c inhibited the folate cycle and the de novo purine biosynthesis tethered to it in cell models, which was associated with activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). Skeletal muscle appeared to be its primary target tissue in mouse experiments [1].

Mitochondrial–nuclear communication

A 2018 study found that MOTS-c translocates to the nucleus under metabolic stress such as glucose restriction, in an AMPK-dependent manner. In the nucleus it was associated with regulation of genes carrying antioxidant response elements and interacted with stress-responsive transcription factors including NRF2 [2]. This made MOTS-c a reference example of a mitochondrial-encoded factor that can act on the nuclear genome.

Muscle signalling models

Later work in cultured myotubes and diet-induced obese mice linked MOTS-c to reduced myostatin signalling through a CK2–PTEN–mTORC2–AKT–FOXO1 pathway [3].

These findings come from cell and animal studies. They describe the biology researchers use MOTS-c to investigate; they are not claims about any effect of a research material in people.

Typical laboratory research questions

  • How mitochondrial-derived peptides signal to the nucleus under metabolic or oxidative stress.
  • AMPK-dependent signalling and folate-cycle metabolism in cultured cells.
  • Comparisons with other mitochondria-targeted research peptides, such as SS-31.

Analytical identity and purity

For a 16-residue peptide, a certificate of analysis should report identity by mass spectrometry (the observed mass should match the expected mass for the sequence above) and purity by reversed-phase HPLC. HPLC area-percent purity is not the same as net peptide content; see our peptide purity guide and how to read a COA. Because MOTS-c contains two methionine residues, oxidation variants (+16 Da) are a relevant impurity class to look for in LC-MS data.

Frequently asked questions

Is MOTS-c the same as humanin or SS-31?

No. Humanin is a different mitochondrial-derived peptide, encoded in the 16S rRNA gene. SS-31 is a synthetic, mitochondria-targeted tetrapeptide, not a mitochondrial-encoded peptide. See SS-31 vs MOTS-c.

What should a MOTS-c certificate of analysis include?

Batch ID, test date, the HPLC purity result with its chromatogram, and an identity result by mass spectrometry that matches the expected mass for MRWQEMGYIFYPRKLR.

Can this material be used in people or animals outside a research protocol?

No. It is sold for laboratory research use only.
